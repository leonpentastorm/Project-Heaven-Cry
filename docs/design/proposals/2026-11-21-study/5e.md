# D&D 5e as a designed curve: brief for PHC (fact-checked)

Fact-check status: proficiency, hit-chance arithmetic, DMG monster table (AC, attack, save DC), 2014 XP thresholds (all 20 levels), the multiplier table, adventuring-day XP and the 2024 High budgets were checked against sources (D&D Beyond Basic Rules 2014 building-encounters page, acodcha's DMG transcription, Blog of Holding, Tom Dunn). Corrections are marked **[corrected]**; opinions presented as fact are marked "(reading)". Items marked **not verified** could not be found online.

Sources used: SRD 5.1 / 2014 Basic Rules; the 2014 DMG, chapters 3, 7 and 9 (page numbers from memory, marked where unsure); Mike Mearls, "Legends & Lore: Bounded Accuracy" (wizards.com, June 2012); Tom Dunn's *The Finished Book* (analysis); Blog of Holding on the 2024 DMG; D&D Beyond posts; bg3.wiki for Baldur's Gate 3. Numbers I could not check online are marked **not verified**.

## 1. Bounded accuracy: the flat axis

**Purpose.** Mearls's 2012 column gave four reasons for bounded accuracy (this four-point list is a paraphrase from memory; **not verified** against the column):
- A low-level monster stays a threat in numbers: orcs still matter at level 10.
- A DC means the same thing at every level, so a DM can set a DC without knowing the party's level.
- The game needs no treadmill where everything rises together and nothing changes in practice.
- The math must not depend on items.

Growth was moved from accuracy into **hit points, damage, action count and options**.

**How it grows.** (verified: SRD 5.1, https://dnd.wizards.com/resources/systems-reference-document)
- Proficiency bonus: +2 at levels 1–4, +3 at 5–8, +4 at 9–12, +5 at 13–16, +6 at 17–20.
- Ability scores cap at 20, so the most an ability adds is +5. A typical start is 16 (+3); ASIs come at 4, 8, 12, 16 and 19 (Fighters and Rogues get extra), so the main ability usually reaches 20 by level 8.
- Attack bonus = proficiency + ability. Save DC = 8 + proficiency + ability.

| Level | Tier | Prof | Typical attack | Typical save DC | Monster AC at CR = level (DMG) | Hit chance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 (1–4) | +2 | +5 | 13 | 13 (CR 1) | 65% |
| 5 | 2 (5–10) | +3 | +7 | 15 | 15 (CR 5) | 65% |
| 11 | 3 (11–16) | +4 | +9 | 17 | 17 (CR 10) | 65% |
| 17 | 4 (17–20) | +6 | +11 | 19 | 19 (CR 17) | 65% |
| 20 | 4 | +6 | +11 | 19 | 19 (CR 20) | 65% |

(Hit-chance column is the brief's own arithmetic and checks out: need 8 or more on the d20 in every row. Note it assumes a +3 ability by level 1 and +5 by level 11, and compares to CR 10 at level 11, not CR 11.)

From level 1 to 20, the whole gain in accuracy is about **+6 on a d20 (30 percentage points)**. Monster AC rises by the same +6. The **hit chance against a level-appropriate foe stays near 65% for the whole career**. Player AC runs from about 16–18 early to 20–22 late (reading; rough typical values, not a rule).

**What that does to low-level monsters.** A CR 1/4 monster attacks at +3 against AC 13. Against a level 11 character at AC 18 it still hits 30%. Hordes stay relevant: a felt sense of power comes from killing them in one hit, not from their missing every time (reading).

**What that does to magic items.** Each +1 is worth 5 percentage points on a d20, which is a lot inside such a narrow band. That is why +N items are rare and capped at +3 (reading: the arithmetic is right, the causal "why" is inferred, not stated by the designers).

## 2. Where growth really comes from

**Hit points.**
- Characters: the first level is the hit die's maximum plus Con; after that, the die's average plus Con each level.
- Monsters: hit points grow about **9×** from CR 1/4 to CR 20 (DMG table), while monster accuracy grows only +7.

| Level | Fighter (d10, Con +2) | Wizard (d6, Con +2) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12 | 8 |
| 5 | 44 | 32 |
| 11 | 92 | 68 |
| 17 | 140 | 104 |
| 20 | 164 | 122 |

**Damage and action count.** These are the real curve (reading). The examples assume a longsword at 1d8 + Str with Str 16 rising to 20, and Fire Bolt.

| Level | Fighter: attacks × average | Fighter damage a round on hits | Fire Bolt | Top spell level (full caster) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 × 7.5 | 7.5 | 1d10 (5.5) | 1st (Burning Hands 3d6, 10.5) |
| 5 | 2 × 8.5 (Extra Attack) | 17 | 2d10 (11) | 3rd (Fireball 8d6, 28 in an area) |
| 11 | 3 × 9.5 | 28.5 | 3d10 (16.5) | 6th |
| 17 | 3 × 9.5, plus Action Surge twice per rest | 28.5+ | 4d10 (22) | 9th |
| 20 | 4 × 9.5 | 38 | 4d10 | 9th, more slots |

**Extra Attack timing.**
- Second attack at level 5: Fighter, Barbarian, Paladin, Ranger and Monk; Valor and Swords Bards at 6; Bladesinger at 6.
- Fighter only: third attack at 11, fourth at 20.
- Cantrips scale with **character level** at 5, 11 and 17, so casters get the same step.

**Class features by level.**
- Level 1: the core identity (Rage, Sneak Attack 1d6, Spellcasting).
- Level 2: secondary features (Action Surge, Cunning Action, Wild Shape).
- Level 3: subclass for most classes. In 2014, Cleric, Sorcerer and Warlock choose at 1, Druid and Wizard at 2 **[corrected: Druid added]**; in 2024, every class chooses at 3.
- Level 5: Extra Attack, 3rd-level spells, proficiency +3. This is the biggest step (reading).
- Level 11: third attack for the Fighter, 6th-level spells, Improved Divine Smite, Reliable Talent.
- Level 17: 9th-level spells, cantrips at 4 dice.
- Level 20: capstone.
- Sneak Attack grows by 1d6 every odd level, from 1d6 to 10d6.

**Why spikes at 5 and 11.** (reading throughout this paragraph: the designers' intent is inferred, not stated in a source I found.) These are tier boundaries, and the design appears to put a doubling of damage at each one. From level 4 to 5 a martial's damage roughly doubles, and Fireball arrives. Tier 2 is described as the point where characters become "heroes" and tier 3 "masters" (DMG chapter 1, the tiers of play; wording **not verified**). The XP table supports this:
- Levels 1–4 pass fast: 300, 900 and 2,700 XP, then 6,500 for level 5. The game is commonly read as intending levels 1–2 to take about one session each (reading; not a stated rule).
- Level 11 needs 85,000 XP, level 17 needs 225,000 and level 20 needs 355,000 (matches the standard table).

**BG3** (bg3.wiki, cap level 12) keeps the same spikes:
- Extra Attack at 5.
- The Fighter's third attack at 11.
- Cantrips scale at 5 and 10 (**the 10 is not verified**).
- Feats at 4, 8 and 12.

BG3's main departure is gear. Uncommon and rare items with on-hit riders, bonus-action effects and stackable small bonuses appear from Act 1, so build depth comes from **item synergies** rather than from a high item bonus (reading). Height gives a +2 attack bonus to ranged attacks (from memory, not checked).

## 3. Choices: ASI against a feat

An ASI is +2 to one ability or +1 to two. At the main ability, +2 gives +1 to hit (about +5 percentage points, or roughly 7–8% more damage at a 65% hit chance) plus +1 damage a hit (about 12% more on a 1d8+3 swing), and +1 to save DCs.

The following is analysis, not rule. Arithmetic by me and by the Finished Book / Treantmonk-style community analyses (the ranges below are **not verified** against a named source):
- At level 4–8, +2 Str adds roughly **15–20% damage a round**.
- Great Weapon Master or Sharpshooter in 2014 (−5 to hit for +10 damage) adds about **25–40%** against AC 15 or lower, and loses damage against high AC unless the character has advantage. These were the 2014 game's biggest gaps between optimised and casual play.
- Polearm Master and Crossbow Expert add a bonus-action attack, worth about +30–50% at levels 4–10.
- A one-level Fighter dip (Fighting Style), or a Hexblade dip for Charisma attacks, lets a build reach "specialist" damage early.

Community white-room numbers put a level 11 optimised martial at roughly **1.5–2× the damage a round of an unoptimised one**. **Not verified** as a single figure; the spread across analyses is wide.

Takeaway (reading): the d20 band stays narrow, and **choices change damage multiplicatively** through actions, riders and trading accuracy for damage, not additively through to-hit.

## 4. Advantage and disadvantage

- Roll two d20s and keep the higher (advantage) or the lower (disadvantage). (verified, SRD 5.1)
- They never stack: any number of sources gives one advantage, and one advantage plus one disadvantage cancel. (verified, SRD 5.1)
- At a 50% need the effect is worth about **+5**, and about +3.3 averaged over all targets (standard probability: 0.5 at a 50% need gives 75%, i.e. +25 points = +5; the +3.3 average is the usual quoted figure, arithmetic not rechecked here). At the extremes it is worth less.
- Reasons for replacing flat bonuses with advantage (reading: widely repeated community and designer commentary, no single primary source found):
  - They keep bookkeeping down.
  - The cap keeps stacked bonuses from breaking bounded accuracy.
  - The DM can rule at the table without arithmetic.
- The remaining flat modifiers are few: cover (+2 / +5), Bless (+1d4) and Bardic Inspiration.
- BG3 applies advantage the same way for height, prone targets and stealth, and its UI shows "advantage" as a state, not a number (from memory, **not verified**).

- **Attunement** caps a character at **3** strong items at once (verified: SRD / DMG). It limits stacking, not acquisition.

## 7. Known failure modes and the 2024 answers

(This whole section is community consensus and the researcher's summary, not rules text: reading. Specific 2024 changes were not rechecked here.)

| Failure | 2024 answer |
|---|---|
| Martial / caster gap ("linear fighter, quadratic wizard"): spells change the scene, attacks only change hit points | Weapon Mastery properties (Topple, Push, Sap, Cleave…); Fighter Tactical Mind and Studied Attacks; Rogue Cunning Strike trades Sneak Attack dice for effects; casters' worst spells nerfed (Conjure, Counterspell now a Con save, Polymorph limits) |
| CR unreliable, especially for solo bosses and against optimised parties | Simpler XP bands with no multiplier; 2024 Monster Manual monsters hit harder and get legendary resistances or actions as needed |
| The 6–8-encounter adventuring day nobody runs, so long-rest classes dominate | The day table dropped; the Warlock gets slots back mid-day; more per-turn and proficiency-bonus-count features than per-rest ones |
| Save-or-suck: one failed save ends a fight | More "save ends" effects, Legendary Resistances, Exhaustion as −2 a level to d20 tests |
| Feat taxes (GWM, Sharpshooter) | −5/+10 removed; GWM now adds proficiency bonus to damage once a turn; Origin feat at 1; Epic Boons at 19 |

## What 5e teaches a squad-tactics game about a felt curve (my reading)

1. **Keep the hit chance flat and make everything else climb.** 5e's 65% against an equal foe at every level is a choice (reading: it falls out of the DMG table and bounded accuracy, but no source says 65% was the target). It frees hit points, damage and actions to grow 5–10× without whiff-fests or auto-hits.
2. **Growth you can feel is a count, not a percentage:** a second attack, a third, a new spell level, a die added to a cantrip. One extra attack a turn is +100%, felt at once; +5% to hit is invisible.
3. **Place spikes at tier boundaries** (levels 5 and 11, about +100% each; Fighter 11 is +50%, not +100%, since 2 to 3 attacks) and let the in-between levels give options, not numbers. Every class should get its spike on the same level.
4. **Make choices change the shape of play.** The biggest 5e gaps between builds came from feats that add an action or change the accuracy-for-damage trade, not from +1s.
5. **One situational modifier that does not stack** (roll two, keep one) keeps tactics legible on a grid, which suits PHC's AP economy.
6. **Gear is a bonus layer with a ceiling** (+3, three attuned items); the base math works without it. BG3 shows that gear depth works when items add riders and synergies, not large flat bonuses.
7. **Monsters scale mostly in hit points and damage**, with accuracy and AC tied to the player curve. Budget encounters in XP per character, but count numbers honestly: the action economy is the real danger, which the 2014 multiplier tried to capture.
8. **Budgeting by "day" fails if the mode does not enforce rests.** A roguelike path map can enforce the day that tabletop 5e never could, so PHC's Survival mode can use resources tied to rests safely.
